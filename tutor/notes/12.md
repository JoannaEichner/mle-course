# Module 12: classification

Lecture: `lectures/12-classification/index.html` (not written yet). Lab: `labs/12-classification/` (not written yet). Code: `src/freshcast/classify/`.

The question of the module: at the end of day `t`, will this product be out of stock for the whole window 06:00-22:00 on day `t + 1`? (`oos_hours == 16` on the next day of the same series.)

## What the learner should own after this module

- A label built with a forward shift is an outcome. The shift goes inside the series, it points at tomorrow, and the last day of every series has no tomorrow: that row is unknown, not negative.
- The rule for dropping unlabelled rows is written down and enforced: a row has no label exactly when it is the last day of its series.
- "Known at forecast time" depends on when the forecast is made. Made after day `t` is over, today is legal. Tomorrow is legal only for what is decided in advance.
- Precision, recall and F1 are four counts and a division, and the zero-division cases are decisions.
- Accuracy says 92% for a model that never raises an alarm. The precision-recall curve and average precision do not.
- A threshold is a business decision. It comes from the two costs, is chosen on validation and reported on test.
- Calibration is a separate property from ranking. Class weights change the probabilities, not the ranking.
- A tree model can learn what a linear one cannot (interactions, thresholds on a feature), and it is compared with a baseline and a linear model on the same rows.

Assumed from earlier modules: per-series shifts and windows on a sorted complete panel (07), time splits and the feature-time rule (08, 09), LightGBM and missing values (10), the test set seen once (08, 11). If `shift` inside a group is shaky, that is the thing to explain, not the task.

## Facts about the data worth having at hand

All measured with the reference code. "City 3" is the `standard` profile, "city 14" the `small` one.

- The design document's "about 4%" is the whole file: 4.04% of 4.5 million rows have `oos_hours == 16`. In the lab profiles it is more: 9.95% of rows in city 3 and 7.50% in city 14. Learners who know "4%" will think something is wrong.
- Labelled rows: 235,672 in city 3 (238,320 minus 2,648 last days), 43,877 in city 14 (minus 493). Base rate of the label: 9.92% and 7.49%.
- The rate falls over time. City 3: 10.2% in train, 9.2% in validation, 8.1% in test. City 14: 7.9%, 5.2%, 6.1%. Validation and test of city 14 hold about 3,450 rows each and only 178 and 212 positives, so every score there moves by several points between weeks. Do not let a learner read a 0.02 difference in city 14 as a result.
- 76% of series in city 3 (83% in city 14) have at least one full-day stockout in the 90 days.
- A full day is a state of its own, not the tail of partial days. City 3: 14 hours 0.67% of rows, 15 hours 0.30%, 16 hours 9.95%.
- Split used in the reference (labelled rows, last 7 days each, the day before a cutoff dropped because its label is the cutoff's first day). City 3: train 193,304 rows (2024-03-28 to 06-08), validation 18,536 (06-10 to 06-16), test 18,536 (06-18 to 06-24). City 14: 35,989, 3,451, 3,451. The days 06-09, 06-17 are purged, 06-25 has no label.
- "Same as today" baseline (predict a full stockout tomorrow if today was one): precision and recall are both about 0.49 in the city 3 test week (0.32 and 0.30 in city 14). They are nearly equal because the number of alarms (stockouts today) and the number of events (stockouts tomorrow) are nearly equal.
- Stockout hours of today are the strongest feature of the trees: 60% of the gain in city 3, 46% in city 14. The second is the share of full days in the last 7 (10% and 17%).
- Calendar of tomorrow (`tomorrow_is_day_off`) carries almost nothing: 0.3% of the gain, and adding or removing it changes average precision by less than 0.01.
- Columns of tomorrow are a leak: `sale_amount` of tomorrow alone lifts LightGBM to average precision 0.93 (city 3) and 0.97 (city 14). See the traps below.
- The first 27 days of every series have no `oos_full_share_28` (30% of the rows), the first 7 days have no `oos_hours_lag_7`. A logistic model that drops rows with NaN loses 30% of its training data; the wrapper fills with the training median and adds indicator columns instead (18 input columns for 11 features).
- The planned discount of tomorrow is the one feature to handle with care. See "Unverified" below.

- Random ranking scores about the base rate in AP (0.08 and 0.06 on test).
- The theoretical threshold for calibrated probabilities is cost_false / (cost_false + cost_missed) = 1/6 = 0.167. The thresholds chosen on validation in city 3 (0.13) are close to it.
- `class_weight="balanced"`: average precision hardly moves (logistic 0.469 to 0.465 in city 3, LightGBM 0.593 to 0.597) but the mean predicted probability on test goes from 0.08 to 0.26 (logistic) and to 0.19 (LightGBM), the optimal threshold moves to about 0.6, and the cost is the same. The probabilities of the weighted logistic model are far above the observed frequencies in every bin: predicted 0.95, observed 0.53 in the top bin.
- The unweighted logistic regression has a mean predicted probability equal to the base rate of its training rows, exactly. The 12.7 check relies on it.
- LightGBM with 400 to 600 trees gains 0.01-0.03 AP on validation in city 3 and loses on test in city 14. The defaults (200 trees, 15 leaves) are a compromise, not an optimum.
- Everything runs in under a second on a quiet machine (LightGBM fit about 0.4 s on 193k rows, `prepare_daily` 0.5 s). When the machine is loaded, LightGBM with all threads can take 20 to 60 s: a slow fit is a load problem before it is a code problem.

### Planned traps, with numbers

Average precision on the test week, same features, LightGBM / logistic. Correct pipeline: city 3 0.593 / 0.469, city 14 0.436 / 0.280.

| Trap | City 3 | City 14 |
|---|---|---|
| Label without grouping by series, last days kept (model trained on correct rows, scored on test plus the last day) | 0.593 to 0.509 | 0.436 to 0.374 |
| Label with the last day set to 0 instead of missing | 0.593 to 0.591 | 0.436 to 0.436 |
| Label shifted the wrong way (yesterday instead of tomorrow) | 1.000 / 0.935 | 1.000 / 1.000 |
| `sale_amount` of tomorrow among the features | 0.930 / 0.898 | 0.968 / 0.954 |
| `sales_in_stock` of tomorrow | 0.945 / 0.924 | 0.987 / 0.974 |
| `oos_hours_total` of tomorrow | 1.000 / 0.999 | 1.000 / 0.998 |
| `sales_while_oos` of tomorrow | 0.612 / 0.468 (no leak) | 0.437 / 0.281 (no leak) |
| No purge of the day before the cutoff: validation AP | 0.578 to 0.608 | 0.533 to 0.560 |
| No purge: test AP | 0.593 to 0.611 | 0.436 to 0.435 |

What the symptoms look like, and why:

- **No grouping.** The last row of every series takes the first day of the next series as its tomorrow. Training is untouched: in a time split the last days are in the test window only. The damage is 1 of 7 test days getting a random label (12.9% positives in city 3): recall at the validation threshold falls from 0.85 to 0.69, and on the last day alone AP is 0.13, which is the base rate. A quiet bug: a worse score, no alarm. Comparing AP per day shows the last day as the outlier.
- **Last day set to 0.** Practically invisible in the score (0.593 to 0.591), because the model has no tomorrow features on that day either and alerts on 8 of 2,648 rows. Only the structural check (a label is missing exactly on the last day) finds it. Use it to argue for assertions over eyeballing scores.
- **Wrong direction.** The label is yesterday's full stockout and `oos_hours_lag_1` is among the features: a perfect score. "Too good" is the symptom, and it appears in both profiles and both models.
- **Columns of tomorrow.** The 0.93-1.00 scores. `oos_hours_total` of tomorrow is 24 hours and contains the 16 of the label; sales in stock say the shelf was empty. `sales_while_oos` is not a leak (sales in hours flagged as out of stock are rare): a learner who tries it first may conclude the idea is safe. Ask them to try all four.
- **Purge.** The row of the day before the cutoff carries the label of the cutoff's first day. Dropping it lowers validation AP by 0.03 in both profiles, a small but consistent inflation. Test AP differs by noise in either direction, so do not promise a test effect.

### Unverified: the discount of tomorrow

The course rule for this task is: from day `t + 1` only the calendar and the planned discount. The reference follows it and includes `discount_tomorrow`. The data cannot settle whether that discount was known at the end of day `t`:

- LightGBM without it: AP 0.593 to 0.533 (city 3), 0.436 to 0.374 (city 14). Logistic regression: no change (0.469 to 0.464, 0.280 to 0.280). It is the single biggest contributor of LightGBM over logistic regression.
- In an ablation on city 3, today's discount adds almost nothing (validation AP 0.482 to 0.490), while tomorrow's adds 0.11 on the same rows. If discounts were stable plans, today's would say much of what tomorrow's says. 51% of rows have no markdown (`discount == 1.0`) on a given day and 45% have none on two days running.
- The share of rows with `discount == 1.0` (no markdown) is 44% on days with 15 stockout hours and 73% on days with 16 (city 3; 51% and 57% in city 14). A plan would not distinguish 15 from 16 hours. A full-day stockout may simply have no recorded markdown.
- Among rows where today's discount is 1.0, the chance of a full stockout tomorrow is 15.0% if tomorrow's discount is 1.0 and 3.4% if it is below 1.

LightGBM stays ahead of logistic regression without the feature, so "LightGBM is stronger" holds either way. If the author decides the discount is not legal for this task, dropping `discount_tomorrow` from `FEATURES` and from `add_stockout_features` is a three-line change, and the check for 12.2 needs its two discount rows removed.

## Tasks

### 12.1 `next_day_value`, `add_stockout_label`, `drop_unlabelled`

- `df["oos_hours"].shift(-1)` without `groupby`. The check shows the symptom: the last day of shop 1 reads 16, the first day of shop 2. Ask: "What is the label of the last row of the first series, and whose data is it?"
- `(tomorrow == 16).astype(int)`: `NaN == 16` is `False`, so the unknown becomes a negative. Ask: "What does your label claim about the last day, and do you know it?" Note that `astype(int)` on a column with NaN raises `IntCastingNaNError`, and the usual next move is `fillna(0)`. Ask why a column of integers cannot say "unknown" and what the options are (float with NaN, nullable integer).
- `shift(1)` instead of `shift(-1)`, or a label that reads "yesterday was a full stockout". Ask: "After your shift, which day's value is in row `t`? Print four rows of one series."
- A threshold such as `>= 15` or `> 0` for "the whole window". The check puts 15 hours in the data. Ask how many hours the window has.
- `dropna` or `fillna(0)` on the label with no rule. `drop_unlabelled` exists to make the rule explicit. Ask: "How many rows did you drop, and which ones should they be?"
- Sorting inside the function so the unsorted input "just works". The docstring says it raises; sorting silently would also break the alignment of the result with the caller's frame.
- Returning a reset index. Later steps align predictions with rows by index.

### 12.2 `rolling_mean_through_today`, `add_stockout_features`

- A window that ends yesterday (reusing `add_rolling_mean` from 07 with its default). Legal, but it throws away the most informative day. The check's message gives the numbers. Ask: "When is this forecast made, relative to the end of today?"
- A window that reaches into tomorrow (`center=True`, or `shift(-1)` before rolling). The check changes outcomes from day `d` on, for several `d`, and compares every earlier row. Ask: "Which of the columns you used will exist at 22:00 tonight?"
- Taking `sale_amount` or `oos_hours_total` of tomorrow because "the lab gives them to me". Same question. After the check passes, have the learner add each as a feature and watch AP go to 0.93-1.00 (see the traps).
- `discount_tomorrow` built from today's discount, or by shifting the wrong way. Ask which day's value sits in the row.
- `rolling` without `groupby`, so the first days of a series average the last days of the previous one. The count of missing values per column in the check catches it.
- `min_periods=1`: partial windows pass as full windows. Ask what `oos_full_share_28` means on day 3.
- Mutating the input (`df["x"] = ...`). Point at `assign`.
- Filling the NaNs here "to help the model". The decision about NaN belongs to the model wrapper in 12.7, where it can be fitted on train only (C4). Ask who else uses this function and what they would want.

### 12.3 `confusion_counts`, `precision`, `recall`, `f1`

- Precision and recall swapped. Ask: "Of all the alarms raised, how many were right? Of all the events, how many were caught? Which denominator is which?"
- F1 as the arithmetic mean. The check uses precision 1, recall 0.5. Ask what a model that alarms on everything would get.
- Zero division: `ZeroDivisionError`, or NaN that then poisons a mean over weeks. The docstring defines 0.0. Ask: "When a model never alarms, what should its precision be, and what would make 1.0 dangerous?" Note that the choice is conventional (it is scikit-learn's default), not a law.
- Not validating the labels. A NaN label (an unlabelled last day) must stop the computation, not become a class. The check passes NaN and the value 2.
- Python loops over rows. Fine for the check, slow on 235k rows. After it passes, time both.
- Computing accuracy and calling it precision. The rare-class example is in the check for this reason: one event in ten days, never alarming, accuracy 0.9.

### 12.4 `counts_by_threshold`, `precision_recall_curve`, `average_precision`

- Treating every row as a threshold, so rows with the same score are split. Ask: "Two rows have the score 0.9, one positive, one not. Which threshold separates them?"
- Thresholds in rising order, which reverses the curve. The docstring fixes falling order, unlike scikit-learn; the lab compares with scikit-learn by reversing and dropping its last point.
- Recall divided by the number of rows instead of the number of events.
- Average precision by the trapezoid rule. The step-wise sum is what scikit-learn computes (the lab shows they agree to 1e-16 on the real data). Ask: "What does interpolating between two points of a precision-recall curve assume?"
- A loop that recomputes the confusion matrix for every threshold: correct, O(n squared). On 18,536 validation rows it is already slow, on 235k unusable. Ask for the cumulative sums after sorting.
- Not failing when there are no positives. Recall is then undefined and the curve means nothing.
- AP of a binary score (the "same as today" baseline) has two thresholds only. It is comparable in kind, not in fairness: compare precision and recall at a chosen threshold as well.

### 12.5 `best_threshold`

- Forgetting the candidate "raise no alarm at all". With costs 3 and 2 the best policy in the check is to stay silent. Ask: "What is the cost of never alarming, and is there a threshold that does that?"
- Ties: the check wants the higher threshold (fewer alarms), and "no alarm" beating a threshold of equal cost. Ask which of two equally cheap policies the operations team would prefer.
- Costs swapped. Ask: "If a missed stockout costs 5 and a false alarm 1, should the threshold be high or low?"
- Choosing on the test set, or evaluating the chosen threshold on the set it was chosen on. Not visible in the check. Ask: "Where did you choose it, and where do you report its cost?" C5.
- Using 0.5. Ask what 0.5 assumes about the two costs. With 5 to 1 and calibrated probabilities the best threshold is near 1/6.

### 12.6 `calibration_table`

- `np.digitize` or `pd.cut` on the default edges: 1.0 lands in its own bin, or 0.5 lands in the lower bin. The check puts 0.5, 1.0 and 0.0 in the data. Ask: "Which bin does a probability of exactly 1.0 belong to?"
- Keeping empty bins as NaN rows. With a rare class most of the upper bins are nearly empty; ask what a bin with 2 rows can tell you. After the check, have the learner print `rows` next to `observed_rate` for the LightGBM probabilities: the highest bin holds 11 rows in the city 3 test week.
- Not failing on probabilities outside [0, 1]. A learner who passes a raw score or a margin gets nonsense bins. The check passes 1.2.
- Reading the table as "the model is wrong". A model can rank well and be miscalibrated. The weighted logistic model is the example.

### 12.7 `StockoutClassifier`

- Fitting the imputer and scaler on all rows in the notebook before the split, instead of inside the wrapper (C4). The check cannot see this; it sees a wrapper whose predictions depend on the batch. Ask: "What does your `predict_proba` do with a single row?"
- Re-fitting anything in `predict_proba`. The check predicts the same row alone and inside a batch of extreme values.
- `dropna` in `fit`: drops the first 27 days of every series (30% of the rows, because of the 28-day window) and the rows with the zero-discount marker, silently. The docstring says the stand-in is the training median plus an indicator. Ask why "no history yet" should be told apart from a typical value.
- Using the mean, or 0, as the stand-in. The check compares a NaN row with a row holding the training median.
- `predict` (hard labels) instead of `predict_proba`, or the two-column output of scikit-learn instead of the positive column.
- `class_weight="balanced"` by default "because the class is rare". Ask: "What does the average predicted probability become? Does the ranking improve?" The numbers are above.
- Using store and product ids as features for the logistic model. Not in the reference; if a learner tries, ask what a numeric id means to a linear model.
- Logistic regression for both kinds. The check has a label that only trees can learn (positive when the signs of two features differ).

## The written questions in the lab

Discuss, do not grade. What a good answer contains:

1. **Accuracy.** A model that never alarms has accuracy 0.919 on the city 3 test week (0.939 in city 14), recall 0 and the highest cost of any non-trivial policy. The 92% is the share of easy rows, not a property of the model.
2. **The baseline.** "Same as today" is right about half of the time (precision 0.49) and catches half of the stockouts. A model has to beat it on the same rows (C6). Its cost is 4,597, against 3,889 for logistic regression and 2,588 for LightGBM, and 7,525 for never alerting. LightGBM cuts the baseline's cost by 44% in city 3, by 15% in city 14 (874 to 745). The last number is a few alarms, so do not over-read it.
3. **The threshold.** An alarm is worth raising when `p * cost_missed > (1 - p) * cost_false`, which for 5 to 1 is `p > 1/6`. The chosen thresholds (0.13 to 0.14 in city 3) are near it, which says the probabilities are roughly calibrated. At 10 to 1 the threshold falls to 1/11, recall rises and precision falls. Answers that only say "lower the threshold to catch more" miss the price.
4. **Class weights.** They raise the predicted probabilities (0.08 to 0.26 on average) without improving the ranking, so the best threshold moves from 0.13 to 0.63 and the cost stays the same. They are a way of moving the threshold with side effects. If the probabilities are used for anything other than ranking, they must be left alone or recalibrated.
5. **Choosing on validation, reporting on test.** The threshold is fitted to the validation rows, so the cost on them is optimistic; the cost on test is the honest one, and it is read once. In city 14 the LightGBM threshold chosen on validation is 0.23, far from 1/6, on 178 positives: a good answer says the choice is noisy there and that more validation weeks would steady it.
6. **The discount.** What would have to be true for tomorrow's discount to be legal? (Set before the forecast is made.) The data cannot say. Ask the learner to run the model without it and report both numbers, and to state in one sentence what they would ask the business.
7. **Why LightGBM wins.** The trees gain from the interaction of stockout history and the discount, which logistic regression cannot express, and from thresholds on hours. Without the discount the gap shrinks (0.593 to 0.533 against logistic's 0.464) but remains.
