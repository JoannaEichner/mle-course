# Module 06: pandas I, cleaning

Lecture and lab: planned as `lectures/06-pandas-cleaning/` and `labs/06-pandas-cleaning/`, not written yet. Code: `src/freshcast/warmup/` (`online_retail.py`, `lines.py`, `daily.py`). Output: `data/processed/online_retail_daily.parquet`, read again by `shelfwise` in module 17 and usable as the input of a weekly forecast.

## What the learner should own after this module

- A column can look right and be wrong: a mixed `int`/`str` column breaks `.str` methods silently, a float hides integers with gaps.
- A missing value is counted before it is handled. Dropping, filling and keeping are each a claim about the data, and the claim goes into a docstring with its number.
- Two kinds of duplicates: copies made by the export (certain, drop) and repeated lines in the source (uncertain, measure, keep and flag). `drop_duplicates()` without a count hides the difference.
- Rows are chosen with `.loc` and a mask, new columns come from `assign`, functions return new frames, a chain reads top to bottom with `pipe`.
- An assumption about the data is a function that raises with a count. A rule is checked against the real file, not only against the example that inspired it.
- An expensive read is done once and cached (xlsx 32 s, parquet 0.04 s).
- After `groupby`, the totals are reconciled with the totals before it.

Assumed from earlier modules: NumPy masks and NaN (05), typed functions with docstrings (02), pytest and `pytest.raises` (04). `groupby` is used once, with a plain sum; module 07 teaches it.

## Facts about the data worth having at hand

All measured on the real file (pandas 3.0, `openpyxl`).

- Two sheets, "Year 2009-2010" (525,461 rows) and "Year 2010-2011" (541,910), 1,067,371 rows together, 8 columns, 53,628 invoices, 5,305 stock codes as written and 5,131 after tidying. Reading the workbook takes 31 s. The parquet cache is 7.2 MB and reads in 0.04 s. The daily result is 2.5 MB.
- Cell types: invoice numbers are numbers in 1,047,871 cells and text in 19,500; stock codes numbers in 932,385 and text in 134,986; customer id is a float column because 243,007 cells are empty (5,942 distinct ids, all whole numbers); 4,382 descriptions are empty and 4 are numbers. Writing the mixed columns to parquet as read fails with `ArrowInvalid` ("Could not convert 'C489449' ... tried to convert to int64"). That is the natural moment to learn why dtypes matter. `.str.startswith("C")` on the mixed column returns NaN for 98% of the rows, without a warning.
- In pandas 3, `astype(str)` on an object column keeps missing values missing. Older tutorials say it produces the text "nan"; the check still guards against it.
- The sheets overlap: 1,088 invoices dated 2010-12-01 to 2010-12-09 are in both, 22,523 lines in each copy, identical line for line.
- Exact duplicate lines in all: 34,335 = 22,523 sheet copies + 11,812 repeats inside one sheet. The 11,812 are in the source: each copy of the overlap contains the same 321 of them. 4,387 invoices hold one; only 1,685 sit directly under their twin. Among the 20,955 invoice-and-product pairs booked on two or more sale lines, 48% have identical quantities; independent draws from each product's own distribution would give 22%. Customers who bought on a repeated line later returned some of the product in 2.5% of the cases, single-line 4.1%. The repeats carry 0.30% of the units and 0.29% of the revenue. The course decision: drop the copies, keep and flag the repeats. Dropping them is defensible too, if argued from these numbers.
- Cancellations: 19,494 lines on 8,292 invoices, all negative quantity except one ("Manual", C496350, quantity 1). Adjustment invoices start with `A`: 6 lines, stock code B, "Adjust bad debt", five of them with a negative price.
- Not a cancellation but negative: 3,457 lines, no customer, price 0, description often empty, "damages", "check", "?", "missing": stock written off. On product codes they are 3,362 lines and 564,030 units, more than the real returns (469,881 units). Treating every negative quantity as a return gives 1,033,911 returned units.
- Price 0 with positive quantity: 2,745 lines (2,576 on product codes, 243,450 units), almost all without customer: samples, "found", "amazon", manual corrections.
- Non-product codes: 61 codes, 6,093 lines. POST 2,122, DOT 1,446, M 1,426, C2 282, D 177, S 104, BANK CHARGES 102, gift vouchers 101, ADJUST 67, AMAZONFEE 43, PADS 19, test products 17, CRUK 16. 158 lines are `DCGS...` dotcom-shop codes, which are real goods with 266 units on 125 sale lines in two years; the five-digits rule drops them knowingly.
- Missing customers: 22.8% of all lines, 22.3% of the sale lines, but 6.1% of the units and 13.1% of the revenue. Median 1 unit per line against 5, median price 3.29 against 1.95, 98.8% United Kingdom against 90.2%. It is per invoice: 8,752 invoices have no id anywhere, 44,876 have it on every line, none is mixed.
- Stock codes in lowercase: 3,471 rows, 183 codes, 173 of which also exist in upper case (161 with the same description). One code has a trailing space.
- Price outliers are not treated: 758 sale lines cost more than ten times their product's median price (0.28% of revenue). Example: 84016 "FLAG OF ST GEORGE CAR FLAG" at 1157.15 pounds against a median of 0.42.
- The two largest sales, 80,995 units of 23843 on 2011-12-09 at 09:15 and 74,215 units of 23166 on 2011-01-18 at 10:01, are cancelled in full 12 and 16 minutes later. Together they are 1.4% of all units sold. Sold and returned are separate columns, so the spike stays in `units_sold` and is cancelled in `units_returned` the same day.
- Trading calendar: 604 of 739 calendar days have lines. One Saturday (2009-12-05), 99 Sundays. No lines from Christmas Eve to 3 January (11 days, both years), so two whole weeks are missing. No lines over Easter (four days). The first day starts at 07:45, the last day (2011-12-09) ends at 12:50: the first and last week are partial.
- Final table: 535,118 rows, 4,721 products, 2009-12-01 to 2011-12-09, 11,221,670 units sold, 469,881 returned, 19,699,784.97 pounds. 5,374 rows are return-only; 14 products have returns and never a sale. Per product: median 69 days with lines and 35 distinct weeks. 3,629 products have sales in at least 13 weeks, 2,834 in at least 26 (92% of units), 1,655 in at least 52 (75% of units).
- Fixtures: `online_retail_sample.xlsx` (40 rows, 8 KB) and `online_retail_sample.parquet` (1,263 rows, 35 KB), rebuilt byte for byte by `tools/make_fixtures_m06.py`.

## Tasks

### 06.1 `read_sheets`, `build_cache`, `load_raw`

- Reading without `dtype` and then trying to write parquet: the error is the lesson. Ask: "Which column does pyarrow object to, and what is in it?"
- `.str.startswith("C")` on the raw mixed column. Ask: "How many True, how many False, how many are neither?"
- `fillna(0)` or `astype(int)` on the customer id. Ask what customer 0 would be and how many rows it would cover (243,007). `errors="coerce"` is the same trap.
- `pd.concat` without `ignore_index=True`: the index repeats 0..525,460. Ask what `raw.loc[0]` returns.
- `pd.concat` of the dict from `sheet_name=None` gives a MultiIndex. Fine if they flatten it; ask what the sheet name is for later (the overlap).
- Tidying the stock code: `upper` without `strip` passes most of the file and fails on one row. The check has that row.
- Before the cache: have them time both reads and say the ratio (about 800 times).
- The cache lives under `data/`, which is ignored by git (rule G4).

### 06.2 `check_raw`

- One big condition with the message "invalid data". Ask: "At 2 a.m., what do you want the message to tell you?"
- Counting cells instead of rows: `isna().sum().sum()` counts a row with two gaps twice. Ask: "Rows or cells?"
- `str.match` instead of `str.fullmatch` for the invoice format: `match` anchors only at the start, so a seven-digit number passes. The check feeds one.
- Rule 6 written as "a negative quantity needs a C invoice". It fails on 3,457 real lines. The check feeds an allowed write-off and a negative price on an A invoice. Ask them to run the rule on the real file before trusting it; a rule that never fires on real data is also suspect.
- Rule 8 with `nunique` over the whole column. Ask for a frame in which each invoice has one customer and the column has three.
- Validation inside the notebook only. Ask where the next person who loads the cache will see it.

### 06.3 `add_flags`

- `is_cancellation = quantity < 0`. Ask: "Of the 22,950 negative rows, how many have an invoice starting with C?" (19,493.) And the one C line with quantity +1.
- A hand-typed list of non-product codes. It works on the lines the learner looked at. Ask how they would find codes they have not seen; the pattern answers it. Watch `85123ABC` and `8512`: the check has both.
- Assigning into the input (`df["is_x"] = ...`). The check reports it; lead to `assign`.
- `price == 0` instead of `<= 0` misses the five adjustment lines; `price < 0` misses 6,202.
- Flags as 0/1 integers or `object`. Ask what `~` does on each.

### 06.4 `drop_sheet_overlap`, `flag_repeat_lines`

- `drop_duplicates()` on the whole table. It passes the obvious test and fails the check because it also removes repeats inside a sheet. Ask first for the count it removes (34,335) and then: "Which of these came from the export, which from the source? How would you tell?" Point at `groupby("invoice")["sheet"].nunique()`.
- Finding the overlap by date range instead of by invoice. It works on this file (both sheets hold the same 2,891 lines for 2010-12-09), but the docstring speaks about invoices and a rule tied to dates breaks as soon as a sheet is cut in the middle of a day.
- Not checking the copies. Ask: "How do you know they are copies?" A good answer compares lines after sorting, in the notebook; the function compares a cheap fingerprint per invoice.
- `sheet` inside the duplicate subset: no copy is ever found. `keep=False` marks the first occurrence too.
- Forgetting `reset_index(drop=True)`.
- The keep-or-drop decision for repeats is argued in the docstring; see the written questions.

### 06.5 `product_lines`

- Returns as `quantity < 0`: 1,033,911 units against 469,881. Ask what the description says on negative lines outside C invoices.
- Dropping missing customers "because they are missing data". Ask what share of units and revenue that removes (6.1%, 13.1%) and what kind of buyer it removes.
- Raising before dropping: the real file has a C line with positive quantity on code M. A version that validates every cancellation fails on real data, the check feeds exactly that row.
- `date` left as a timestamp: one row per invoice time instead of per day. The check sees it. Ask how many groups `groupby` would make.
- Negative revenue for returns, or revenue net of returns. The contract says gross revenue of sale lines; ask what cannot be computed from it (net revenue) and what the contract costs.
- Filtering with chained indexing and getting `SettingWithCopyWarning` (rule D9). The answer is `.loc` with a mask, or building the result from the filtered frame.

### 06.6 `daily_totals`, `check_daily`

- `groupby` without `dropna=False`: lines with a missing key vanish without a trace. The check plants one. Ask: "Do the totals after the groupby equal the totals before it?" Reconcile 11,221,670 sold and 469,881 returned on the real file.
- Summing everything, including text columns, instead of the three measures.
- `mean` or `count` instead of `sum`. The check compares the numbers.
- Units become floats after a merge or `fillna`. Ask why integers matter for a count of units.
- Checking dtypes with `== "int64"`; it rejects nullable integers that would do. Either is accepted by the check as long as it is an integer type.
- Sortedness checked by comparing with a sorted copy: fine. Ask what a sorted panel is needed for in module 07.
- The rule "a row has a sale or a return" looks odd until they see what a full outer join would produce.

### 06.7 `build_daily`, `save_daily`, `load_daily`

- `check_raw` returns nothing, so it cannot sit in a `pipe` chain. Ask why a validator should return nothing, and what the alternatives cost.
- Checking the contract only on save. Ask: "A colleague changes the columns tomorrow. What happens when you load the file you wrote last week?"
- Saving first and checking after: a bad file is left on disk. The check looks for the file.
- `to_parquet` without `index=False`: the round trip differs by an index column.
- Skipping `drop_sheet_overlap` or using `drop_duplicates` in the chain: the sample totals differ (7,368 and 6,960 against 7,075 units sold). Let the learner find the difference by comparing with their own step-by-step result.

## Exploration questions (lab, ungraded)

Provisional until the lab exists. What a good answer contains:

1. **Is a repeated line an error?** It says the file cannot settle it. It names at least two pieces of evidence: the repeats are in both copies of the overlap, so the source made them; identical quantities are twice as common as independence would give (48% against 22%), so some are re-keyed; customers do not return them more often (2.5% against 4.1%), so there is no sign of billing errors. It names the cost: 0.30% of the units either way. It says why flagging beats dropping: it can be undone.
2. **Who are the customers without an id?** Not a random hole: whole invoices, small quantities, higher unit prices, almost all United Kingdom, 22.3% of sale lines but 6.1% of units. Dropping them would shift demand towards wholesale.
3. **What does a missing day mean?** Not zero demand. The shop did not trade on Saturdays, between Christmas and 3 January, or over Easter. Filling every gap with zero would invent two weeks of zero sales for every product. The first and last week are partial.
4. **What do the two biggest sales do to the data?** Each is cancelled within a quarter of an hour, so net units are unaffected but `units_sold` spikes, and a model of gross demand would learn a spike that never reached a customer. Good answers propose a decision for the forecasting step and say it is not made here.
5. **What was not cleaned, and why?** Price outliers (0.28% of revenue), descriptions, countries. A good answer says each stays out because nothing in the daily table depends on it, and what would change that.
