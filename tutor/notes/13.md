# Module 13: unsupervised learning and a first neural network

Lecture: `lectures/13-*/index.html` (slug set by the lecture author). Lab: `labs/13-*/`. Code: `src/freshcast/segments.py`, `src/freshcast/models/mlp.py`.

## What the learner should own after this module

- Clustering has no target, so every choice (what to describe, how to scale, how many clusters) needs evidence the learner reads and can defend. "The algorithm found 6 groups" is never the evidence.
- k-means measures distances: scale first, fix the seed, keep several initialisations, and know that cluster numbers are arbitrary names.
- Inertia always falls with k; silhouette says how separated the clusters are. A silhouette near 0.3 means there are no natural groups, only a partition of a continuum.
- PCA shows the two directions of largest spread. Scale before PCA when the columns have different units, only centre when they share one (hourly shares).
- A feature built from clusters is fitted on training data only, like a scaler. The honest test has a control (random labels) next to the baseline.
- A neural network is tensors, an `nn.Module`, a loss, an optimiser and a loop the learner writes: forward, loss, `zero_grad`, `backward`, `step`. Early stopping keeps the weights of the best epoch, not the last.
- On tabular data like this a small MLP does not beat LightGBM; the learner can say why and show one concrete failure (extrapolation).

Assumed from earlier modules: standardising with training statistics only (08), `PanelForecaster`, the feature table and LightGBM (09, 10), `rolling_origin_folds` and `backtest` with `fit_mask` (11), `row_shares` and `to_matrix` (05, 07). If the C4 rule (fit only on training rows) is shaky, that is the thing to explain, not the task.

## Facts about the data worth having at hand

All numbers measured on the processed panel, 4 rolling-origin folds of 7 days, training on rows with `oos_hours == 0` and `sale_amount_lag_28` present, score `wape_in_stock`.

- Profile `standard` (city 3): 2,648 series. Profile `small` (city 14): 493 series. No series has zero sales on all days in either city.
- Behaviour numbers (city 3, median): cv 0.64, weekend uplift +0.21, discounted share 0.46, stockout rate 0.18, zero-sale days 5.6%. The profile columns are heavily correlated in one pair: cv and zero share (0.84), stockout rate and zero share (0.63). Stockouts and zero-sale days are largely the same phenomenon.
- Mean sales is heavy-tailed: city 3 from 0.35 to 4.1, city 14 from 0.38 to 16.9. In city 14 seven series of the "always zero discount" product (module 07) sell about 13.6 a day, 18% of the city's volume. With raw mean sales k-means gives them their own cluster of 7 at k=3 and 4. The reference uses the natural log of the mean, which removes this.
- The discount of a few rows is NaN (103 rows in city 3, 623 in city 14, nearly all in those 7 series). The reference counts them as "not discounted", so those 7 series have `discounted_share` 0.
- Inertia, city 3, k = 1..10: 15,888, 11,289, 9,607, 8,354, 7,265, 6,370, 5,706, 5,319, 5,014, 4,687. No elbow: every step is a bit smaller than the last. City 14: 2,958, 2,291, 1,898, 1,622, 1,399, 1,273, 1,155, 1,041, 970, 868.
- Silhouette, k = 2..10, city 3: 0.322, 0.228, 0.249, 0.254, 0.271, 0.266, 0.257, 0.263, 0.241. City 14: 0.377, 0.230, 0.244, 0.259, 0.263, 0.271, 0.287, 0.281, 0.273. The maximum is k=2 in both, and it is weak. After that the curve is flat; a local maximum sits at k=6 in city 3 and k=8 in city 14.
- What the clusters are. k=2, city 3: 1,852 steady series (cv 0.58, stockout rate 0.18, 5% zero days) and 796 intermittent ones (cv 0.98, stockout rate 0.34, 26% zero days). City 14: 432 and 61, same split. k=6, city 3: intermittent with few discounts (570), steady and heavily discounted (636), steady big sellers (332), steady and rarely discounted (880), very erratic (135), strong weekend uplift of +0.73 (95). Cluster numbers differ from run to run.
- Stability: the adjusted Rand index between seed 0 and seeds 1, 2, 3 is 0.98, 0.98, 0.99 for k=6 in city 3, but 0.64, 0.97, 1.0 for k=8 in city 14. More clusters on fewer series give less stable clusters.
- PCA, two components: the six behaviour numbers carry 57.4% of the variance in city 3 and 57.8% in city 14. The 24-hour sales shares per series carry 52.5% (city 3) and 55.2% (city 14). The first hourly component is "morning against evening": positive on hours 6 to 10, negative on hours 16 to 21.
- Hourly profile caveat: hours without stock have zero sales, so a product that sells out at noon looks like a morning product. In city 3 there are 70 series with no fully stocked day with sales (20 in city 14).
- LightGBM with the course features (categorical `store_id` and `product_id` included): city 3 0.3768, city 14 0.2869. Numeric features only: 0.4205 and 0.3014. The two id columns are worth about 0.04 and 0.015.
- Segment as a feature for LightGBM, k=6 in city 3 (k=8 in city 14), honest (fitted on the days before each fold):
  - with the id columns: city 3 0.3760 against 0.3768 without, city 14 0.2831 against 0.2869;
  - random labels with the same number of levels (five draws) give 0.3769 to 0.3780 in city 3 and 0.2840 to 0.2854 in city 14: a column of noise moves the score by up to 0.003, and the honest segment beats the best random draw by only about 0.001;
  - numeric features only: city 3 0.4002 against 0.4205 (random labels 0.4197 to 0.4213), city 14 0.2982 against 0.3014 (random 0.3009 to 0.3020);
  - segments fitted on the whole panel before the backtest ("leaky") look slightly better still: 0.3745 and 0.3975 in city 3.
  So the segment helps where it stands in for the series identity the model lacks, and adds almost nothing once `store_id` and `product_id` are there.
- The MLP, default settings (64-32 hidden units, Adam 3e-3, batches of 256, stop after 10 epochs without gain on the last 7 training days): city 3 0.4409, 0.4512, 0.4505 for seeds 0, 1, 2, about 5 s per fold on 4 threads, 17 to 39 epochs run, best epoch between 6 and 28. City 14: 0.4287, 0.4175, 0.4000, about 0.7 s per fold. LightGBM takes under 1 s per fold in both.
- Why the MLP is worse. It has no series identity (numeric features only), and in city 14 it extrapolates: in the last fold the mean temperature of the validation week is 3 standard deviations above the training mean and 88% of its rows are warmer than any training day. Trees stop at the warmest leaf they know, the network keeps going. Without the four weather columns the MLP scores 0.305 to 0.317 in city 14 (LightGBM numeric 0.294) and the bias flips from about +0.2 to -0.05. In city 3 it makes no difference (0.437 to 0.448).
- Training rows with a missing value: in every fold 11% to 18% of the training rows lack the two 28-day window features (they have lag 28 but not yet 34 days of history). Dropping them would shrink the first fold by 18%; the forecaster flags and imputes instead.
- Early stopping on one week is noisy. The training loss falls steadily (0.29 to 0.16 in 40 epochs) while the validation loss bounces around 0.22 to 0.25 with no trend. At a learning rate of 1e-3 the best epoch was 6, 1, 8 and 30 in the four folds.
- Six MLP variants on city 3 (64-32 at 1e-3, 128-64, 3e-3, batch 1024, 32-16, 64-64-32) all land between 0.441 and 0.461. No variant comes near LightGBM numeric (0.4205). Do not let the learner tune their way to a different conclusion in an hour.

## Tasks

### 13.1 `series_profile`

- Global statistics instead of per-series ones (`df["sale_amount"].std()`, uplift against the global mean). The check puts two very different series in one frame. Ask: "If I add a third series, may the first one's numbers change?"
- `Series.std()` in pandas divides by n-1, NumPy by n. The docstring says `ddof=0`. Ask which one they used and whether the difference matters with 90 days (0.6%; with the 7 days of the check, 8%).
- Weekend taken from `holiday_flag`. That flag is "day off in China": weekends plus six workday holidays, and Sunday 2024-04-07 was a working day. Ask: "Does `holiday_flag == 1` mean Saturday or Sunday? Count the rows where it does not."
- `stockout_rate` divided by 24. `oos_hours` counts 06:00 to 22:00, so 16. Ask which hours the column covers (module 07).
- Comparing a NaN discount: `NaN < 1` is False. Ask how many rows that touches and what the profile of the always-zero product then says about it. Dropping the NaN days instead changes the share and the check reports it.
- Mean sales on the raw scale. The column is named `log_mean_sales`, so the check enforces the log; the reason is in the data. In the lab let them cluster a raw version once in city 14 and ask why seven series form a cluster.
- Result indexed by position instead of the series key, so labels cannot be joined back to the panel. Ask how they would attach the segment to the daily rows.

### 13.2 `standardise`, `fit_segments`

- k-means on unscaled columns. The check has a column in thousands of pure noise next to a clean two-group column. Ask: "Which column decides the distance between two series?"
- A constant column: division by a standard deviation of zero gives NaN. Ask what a column that never changes can say about groups.
- Fitting the scaler on the profile and then again on new series. A new product appears next week: how does it get a segment? Answer: `scaler.transform`, then the nearest centre; never refit. This is rule C4 again.
- No `random_state`, or `n_init` left at the default. Ask them to run twice and compare label vectors; then compare with the adjusted Rand index, not with `==`, because the numbers are arbitrary names.
- Expecting equal-size or round clusters. k-means minimises squared distance to a centre; say what that assumes.
- Mutating the input profile while scaling (writing the scaled values back into the frame).

### 13.3 `inertia_by_k`, `silhouette_by_k`

- Choosing k with the lowest inertia. Ask what k = number of series gives.
- Looking for an elbow that is not there. Both curves are in the facts above: a smooth decline and a flat silhouette. The good answer says so, names k=2 as the strongest signal, calls it weak (0.32), and picks a larger k for a stated reason (what is it for: forecasting needs more than "steady" and "intermittent") while admitting the data does not demand it.
- Silhouette for k=1 or k=n is undefined. The check expects a `ValueError`; sklearn raises one too, so a learner who does not validate still passes. That is fine.
- Silhouette is quadratic in the number of series: fine for 2,648, slow for the 50,000 of the full profile. Ask what they would do (sample).
- Different seeds inside the two functions and in `fit_segments`, so the curve and the final model disagree. The module constant `N_INIT` exists so that they do not.

### 13.4 `hour_profiles`, `project_2d`

- Mean of daily shares instead of shares of summed sales. The check's two days have different totals. Ask: "A day with 1 sale and a day with 100: should they weigh the same?"
- Looping over series. The groupby-sum is one line; ask what the loop costs for 50,000 series.
- Scaling the hourly shares before PCA. Night hours have tiny variance; scaled, they weigh as much as the morning peak. The shares already share a unit.
- Not centring (when PCA is written by hand with `svd`). The check shifts the data by 100.
- Reading the picture: the sign of a component is arbitrary. "PC1 is morning against evening" is the claim; "high PC1 means a morning product" holds only for one sign convention. Ask them to name two series at opposite ends and look at their raw profiles.
- Reading the explained variance: two components carry about half of the variance. The picture leaves out the other half; say what cannot be concluded from it.
- Stockouts flatten the profile of a product that sells out (the caveat in the facts). Ask whether a product that sells out at noon every day is a morning product or a supply-constrained one.

### 13.5 `TabularMLP`

- Forgetting `super().__init__()`: the error says a module cannot be assigned before `Module.__init__()`. Explain what `nn.Module` registers.
- Layers in a plain Python list. `model.parameters()` is then empty, and the optimiser raises or, worse, nothing learns. Ask: "What does `len(list(model.parameters()))` print?"
- A ReLU after the last layer: the output is never below zero and the forecast for a day of zero sales is stuck. Ask what a hidden layer without an activation does (two linear layers are one linear layer).
- `forward` returning shape `(n, 1)`. It passes everything until the loss: `MSELoss` of `(n, 1)` against `(n,)` broadcasts to `(n, n)` and warns. The loss is then computed over every pair of rows and the network learns the mean. Let them fall in (the check on 13.5 reports the shape; if they skip it, 13.6 shows a flat loss). Ask them to read the warning text.
- Parameter count: ask them to derive 29 for 3-4-2-1 by hand before printing it.

### 13.6 `train_mlp`

- `float64` arrays into a float32 network: "mat1 and mat2 must have the same dtype". Explain the default dtype of PyTorch and why GPUs like float32.
- `torch.from_numpy` shares memory with the array; an in-place change of the tensor changes the caller's data. The check compares the arrays before and after.
- Forgetting `optimiser.zero_grad()`: gradients add up across batches and the loss misbehaves. Ask what `.grad` holds after two `backward()` calls.
- `best = model.state_dict()` without a copy. `state_dict()` returns references to the live tensors, so "best" silently follows the model and the restore does nothing. The check compares the returned model's validation loss with the minimum in the history and fails. Ask: "What does `id()` of the tensors say before and after a step?"
- Patience off by one: stopping when the counter exceeds `patience`, or resetting it on a tie. The check pins the length of the history to `best epoch + 1 + patience`.
- Storing `loss` instead of `loss.item()`: the history holds the whole graph and memory grows. Ask them to watch memory over 100 epochs.
- Validation without `torch.no_grad()` and `model.eval()`: here the numbers come out the same, because the network has no dropout, but they will meet both in module 18. Explain the habit now.
- Averaging batch losses without weighting by batch size, so the smaller last batch counts as much as a full one. Small effect; the check cannot see it. Ask what the training loss would be if the last batch had one row.
- No shuffling. The check cannot see it, and the real table is sorted by series then day, so each batch would hold a handful of series. Ask them to train once without `shuffle=True` on the real data and compare the validation curve.
- Reading the loss curves: train loss keeps falling, validation loss does not. That is the overfitting picture; ask what would regularise it (weight decay, dropout, fewer units, less training) and why early stopping is the cheapest of these.
- Seeds: `torch.manual_seed` is global and separate from NumPy's. Two runs of the same seed match on one machine; across machines or thread counts tiny differences are normal.
- Tiny batches on many threads are slow and can be slower than on one thread, especially on a loaded machine. `torch.set_num_threads` is the knob; it does not belong in the package.

### 13.7 `MLPForecaster`

- Scaling statistics computed in `predict` (from the rows to forecast) or on all rows including the held-out week. The check makes a row's forecast independent of the other rows, and trains on a frame where the last week differs by 1000.
- `fillna(0)` before scaling: 0 is not the mean of a column and `np.mean` with a NaN returns NaN. The reference uses the training mean (zero after scaling) and adds a 0/1 input per column that had missing values in training. The check makes missingness itself informative (those rows sell about 40 more) and expects the network to find it. Ask: "What can the network tell apart with the flag that it cannot without it?"
- A NaN at `predict` in a column that never had one in training is an error, not a guess. Ask which is worse for a forecast, a crash or a number from an input the network never saw.
- Holding out rows at random instead of the last days. The validation loss then looks good and says nothing about next week. The check puts a +1000 shift on the last `valid_days` days: a correct hold-out leaves the validation loss near 1e6 while training loss goes below 100.
- `y.loc[train.index]` assumes a unique index; `backtest` passes one. Ask what breaks with a duplicated index.
- Passing `store_id` and `product_id` as numbers. They are labels, not quantities (module 09). The course's numeric feature list leaves them out, and that costs about 0.04 WAPE against LightGBM. Ask what an embedding would give, and point at module 18.
- Comparing with LightGBM on unequal terms: different folds, different training mask, all rows instead of in-stock rows. Require the same `backtest` call, the same `fit_mask`, and LightGBM with numeric features only next to the full one.
- Drawing conclusions from a single seed. The spread between seeds is about 0.01 in city 3 and 0.03 in city 14. Ask for three seeds.
- Training rows lost to NaN: if the learner drops rows with any NaN instead of flagging, the first fold loses 18% of its training rows (10,004 of 54,210). Ask how many rows the fold then has.

## Written questions in the lab

Discuss, do not grade. What a good answer contains:

1. **Did the segment help?** The honest answer has three parts: with the id columns the gain (0.0008 in city 3, 0.004 in city 14) is about 0.001 more than the best of five random labelings gives, too little to claim; without them k=6 gains 0.02 in city 3, which is real (random labels give nothing) and still leaves the model behind one that simply uses `store_id` and `product_id`; segments fitted before the split look a little better than honest ones, and the direction of the error is the point, not its size.
2. **Why must the segments be fitted inside each fold?** The profile of a series contains its sales in the validation week (its mean, its zero days, its weekend uplift). A feature built from it tells the model something about the answer. A scaler fitted on all rows is the same mistake in a smaller form.
3. **What does the first hourly component show?** Morning peak against evening peak; say which hours load positively and negatively, that the sign is arbitrary, and that about half the variance is left out.
4. **Are there clusters in the series?** No clear ones: silhouette 0.32 at best, no elbow. The segments are a convenient partition of a continuum (regular against intermittent, discount policy, a few heavy sellers), and different seeds give different cuts at larger k.
5. **Why does LightGBM beat the MLP here?** Named causes, each with evidence from the lab: the identity columns (0.04), extrapolation on temperature in the last fold of city 14 (remove the weather and the gap closes), a noisy stop on one week, inputs spanning very different scales, and seeds that move the score by 0.01. A weak answer says "trees are just better" or "the network needs more data".
6. **What would you try next?** Good: an embedding for the series id, predicting a ratio to the series level instead of raw sales, clipping inputs to the training range, weight decay or dropout, more than one week to stop on. Each with the reason it addresses one of the causes above.
